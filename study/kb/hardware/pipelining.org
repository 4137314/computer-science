#+title: Pipelining
#+summary: Inserting \(k-1\) register stages into a feed-forward circuit of delay \(D\) splits it into \(k\) stages of about \(D/k\): the clock period, and hence the throughput, improves almost \(k\) times, while the latency grows to \(k\) cycles and every stage adds register overhead.

* Construction
For a feed-forward path (no loops), add registers on a *cut* that separates inputs from outputs, crossing every path once; then [[id:retiming][retime]] to balance the stages. With logic delay \(D\) and register overhead \(t_{reg}=t_{cq}+t_{su}\):
\[ T_k = \frac{D}{k} + t_{reg},\qquad \text{throughput}=\frac{1}{T_k},\qquad \text{latency}=k\,T_k = D + k\,t_{reg}. \]

* Derivation
Without pipelining one result leaves every \(D+t_{reg}\). With \(k\) balanced stages a new input enters every \(T_k\), and \(k\) operations are in flight at once. The speed-up of throughput is \((D+t_{reg})/(D/k+t_{reg})\to (D+t_{reg})/t_{reg}\) as \(k\to\infty\): register overhead caps the gain, and latency only grows.

* Intuition
An assembly line: each worker does a slice of the job. Cars come out more often; each car still spends as long as before in the factory, a bit longer.

* Pitfall
Loops cannot be pipelined this way: a result needed in the next cycle (an accumulator, a branch decision) cannot wait \(k\) cycles. Processor pipelines must handle such dependences (hazards) explicitly.
