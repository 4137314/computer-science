#+title: Delta cycles
#+summary: Zero-delay assignments are not instantaneous in simulation: each takes one delta cycle, an infinitesimal step at the same simulated time; the simulator alternates "update signals" and "run woken processes" until no event is left at that time, which orders cause and effect without real delay.

* Model
A simulation cycle at time \(t\):
1. *update*: apply all transactions scheduled for \((t,\delta)\); a signal whose value changes has an event;
2. *evaluate*: run every process (and concurrent assignment) sensitive to a signal with an event; their assignments without =after= are scheduled for \((t,\delta+1)\);
3. if anything is scheduled for \((t,\delta+1)\), repeat at the next delta; otherwise advance to the next time with a scheduled transaction.
Simulated time is the pair \((t,\delta)\), ordered lexicographically; \(\delta\) steps take no simulated time.

* Derivation
For =c <= a and b; e <= c or d;= with =a= rising from 0 to 1 at time 10 ns while =b='1':
- \((10,0)\): =a= changes; =c <= a and b= is evaluated and schedules =c='1' at \((10,1)\);
- \((10,1)\): =c= changes; =e <= c or d= runs and schedules =e='1' at \((10,2)\);
- \((10,2)\): =e= changes; nothing is sensitive to it: time advances.
In a waveform viewer =c= and =e= both change "at 10 ns", but =e= is after =c=.

* Intuition
Delta cycles are the simulator's way of saying "after, but not later": they keep causality in a world where gates have no delay.

* Pitfall
A combinational loop with zero delay (=x <= not x;=) never runs out of delta cycles: the simulator stops with an iteration-limit error at a fixed simulated time.
