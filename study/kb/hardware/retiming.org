#+title: Retiming
#+summary: Moving registers across combinational gates — from all inputs of a gate to its output or back — does not change the input–output behaviour seen at the cycle level but changes the length of the register-to-register paths, so the clock period can be reduced (or the number of registers minimised).

* Method
Model the circuit as a graph: nodes are combinational blocks with delay \(d(v)\), edges carry a weight \(w(e)\) = number of registers on them. A retiming assigns an integer lag \(r(v)\) to each node: \(r(v)\) registers are moved from its outputs to its inputs. The new weights are
\[ w_r(u\to v)=w(u\to v)+r(v)-r(u), \]
legal if all \(w_r\ge0\). Around any cycle the total weight is unchanged (the lags cancel), so loops keep their latency, and the cycle-level behaviour is preserved. The clock period is the longest path with zero registers; Leiserson and Saxe find the minimum period by testing candidate periods, each check being a system of difference constraints solved as shortest paths.

* Intuition
Registers are checkpoints; retiming moves them along the road so that no stretch between two checkpoints is too long.

* Pitfall
Moving registers changes their initial (reset) states: an equivalent reset state may not exist after a backward move, and the registers no longer correspond to the names in the RTL, which complicates debugging and verification.
